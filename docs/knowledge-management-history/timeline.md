# Timeline: From Memex to AI Knowledge Management

A chronological history of knowledge management systems, ideas, and movements from 1945 to the present.

## Pre-History and Conceptual Foundations (Pre-1945)

- **~8000 BCE:** Record-keeping begins in Mesopotamia using clay tokens — the earliest known knowledge externalization technology.
- **~300 BCE:** Library of Alexandria established as the ancient world's most ambitious knowledge repository.
- **~1440:** Gutenberg's printing press revolutionizes knowledge dissemination, making books standardized and broadly accessible.
- **1920s:** Emanuel Goldberg at Zeiss Ikon pioneers electronic document retrieval — a largely forgotten precursor to Bush's memex. [Source: [Goldberg, Electronic Document Retrieval, and Bush's Memex - Berkeley](https://people.ischool.berkeley.edu/~buckland/goldbush.html)]

## The Visionary Era (1945–1969)

### 1945 — Vannevar Bush publishes "As We May Think"

Bush describes the **memex** — a hypothetical device for storing, linking, and retrieving personal knowledge. Key features: associative trails connecting documents, marginal annotations, and flexible indexing. The essay, published in *The Atlantic*, directly inspires the hypertext movement. **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** This is the most widely cited conceptual origin of modern knowledge management technology.

[Source: [As We May Think - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_We_May_Think); [Still Building the Memex - CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/still-building-the-memex/)]

### 1956 — White-collar workers outnumber blue-collar workers in the US

A demographic inflection point: knowledge work becomes the dominant form of labor, creating the economic conditions that eventually demand KM systems.

### 1959 — Peter Drucker coins "knowledge worker"

In *The Landmarks of Tomorrow*, Drucker identifies knowledge as the central resource of modern economies and predicts that managing knowledge workers will require fundamentally different approaches than managing industrial labor. He argued knowledge workers function as "executives" — making strategic decisions, not just following orders.

[Source: [Peter Drucker: The Rise of Knowledge Workers - Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/peter-drucker-knowledge-worker/); [Drucker - The Knowledge Worker 1966 - KnowledgeJump](https://www.knowledgejump.com/history_knowledge/drucker.html)]

### 1962 — Douglas Engelbart publishes "Augmenting Human Intellect"

Engelbart articulates a framework for using computers to augment human intellectual capabilities — not replace them. This becomes the philosophical foundation for NLS and much subsequent KM work.

### 1965 — Ted Nelson coins "hypertext"

Nelson's concept of interconnected, non-linear text builds directly on Bush's memex vision and eventually becomes a core technology for knowledge management systems.

### 1968 — "The Mother of All Demos"

Engelbart publicly demonstrates NLS (oN-Line System) at a Stanford conference, showcasing the mouse, hypertext links, screen windowing, collaborative editing, and video conferencing — all technologies that would become fundamental to KM systems decades later.

[Source: [NLS Computer System - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NLS_(computer_system)); [1968 Demo Interactive - Doug Engelbart Institute](https://www.dougengelbart.org/firsts/1968-demo-interactive.html)]

## The Expert Systems Era (1970s–1980s)

### 1965–1983 — DENDRAL and early expert systems

DENDRAL, developed at Stanford, becomes the first successful expert system, establishing the principle of separating knowledge bases from inference engines. This "knowledge principle" — that knowledge itself is the source of problem-solving power — becomes foundational.

[Source: [DENDRAL History - MIT](https://web.mit.edu/6.034/www/6.s966/dendral-history.pdf)]

### 1970s — Drucker and Strassman formalize knowledge as corporate asset

Peter Drucker and Paul Strassman argue that information and human expertise should be treated as formal corporate assets, laying the management foundation for KM as a discipline.

[Source: [Knowledge Management History - Bloomfire](https://bloomfire.com/blog/knowledge-management-history/)]

### 1976 — MYCIN expert system

MYCIN, developed at Stanford for diagnosing bacterial infections, demonstrates both the promise and limitations of rule-based knowledge capture. It achieved diagnostic accuracy comparable to human experts but was never deployed clinically.

### 1980s — Expert systems boom

Corporations invest heavily in expert systems. Companies like Lisp Machines and Symbolics develop specialized AI hardware. The field promises to capture expert knowledge in rule-based systems accessible to non-experts.

**Key lesson that emerges:** The "knowledge engineering bottleneck" — experts cannot fully articulate their tacit knowledge, knowledge engineers misunderstand domain nuances, and the resulting systems are brittle and expensive to maintain.

[Source: [Expert System - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_system); [Knowledge Base Development Challenges - Columbia/Buchanan-Shortliffe 1984](https://people.dbmi.columbia.edu/~ehs7001/Buchanan-Shortliffe-1984/Chapter-08.pdf)]

### 1986 — Peak of expert systems hype

AI conference attendance peaks. Expert systems are being sold as business solutions for customer relationship management, demand prediction, and operational optimization.

### 1987 — The Second AI Winter begins

Expert systems fail to deliver on commercial promises. The qualification problem (inability to encode all possible real-world conditions) proves insurmountable. Specialized AI hardware cannot compete with cheaper general-purpose computers. Funding collapses.

**[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** The AI Winter is the first major cycle of technology overpromise and disillusionment in knowledge management. Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems prove more practical for the business problems expert systems claimed to solve.

[Source: [The Second AI Winter 1987-1993 - Holloway](https://www.holloway.com/g/making-things-think/sections/the-second-ai-winter-19871993); [How the AI Boom Went Bust - CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/opinion/how-the-ai-boom-went-bust/)]

### 1987 — McKinsey coins "knowledge management"

McKinsey & Company conducts an internal study on information handling and uses the term "knowledge management" in its modern organizational sense.

[Source: [Knowledge Management History - Bloomfire](https://bloomfire.com/blog/knowledge-management-history/)]

## First Wave: Technology-Centric KM (1990s)

### 1991 — Nonaka and Takeuchi publish the SECI model

The Socialization-Externalization-Combination-Internalization model becomes the dominant theoretical framework for understanding knowledge creation and conversion. It describes a spiral process for converting tacit knowledge to explicit and back.

**[CONTESTED]** The SECI model faces significant criticism: empirical basis is questioned, socialization and combination may be transfer rather than true conversion, and the model is often applied beyond its conceptual limits. [Source: [Critical Evaluation of Nonaka's SECI Model - Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-71434-9_3); [SECI Empirical Shortcomings - Kingston University](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/id/eprint/2291/)]

### 1991 — Lave and Wenger publish "Situated Learning"

Communities of practice concept is introduced, arguing that learning happens through social participation rather than knowledge transfer. This becomes a major alternative to technology-centric KM.

[Source: [Evolution of Wenger's Community of Practice - Implementation Science](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2654669/)]

### Mid-1990s — First generation KM tools proliferate

Intranets, Lotus Notes, document management systems, and early knowledge repositories spread through enterprises. The dominant paradigm is "supply-side KM" — getting the right information to the right people at the right time.

**[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** Implementation success rates for these systems hovered around 30%, comparable to other enterprise information systems. Technology was prioritized over people and process.

[Source: [Generations of KM - DKMS](https://dkms.com/papers/generationsofkm.pdf); [KM Technology and Reproduction - ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0963868700000433)]

### 1995 — Nonaka and Takeuchi publish *The Knowledge-Creating Company*

This becomes the seminal text of the KM field, arguing that organizational knowledge creation is the key to Japanese firms' competitive success.

### Late 1990s — KM becomes mainstream business practice

Most large companies adopt some form of KM initiative. Knowledge management positions (Chief Knowledge Officer) emerge. Consulting firms heavily promote KM solutions.

## Second Wave: People-Centric KM (2000s)

### 1999 — Tim Berners-Lee proposes the Semantic Web

The vision: a web where machines understand meaning, enabling intelligent knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries. Technologies: RDF, OWL, ontologies.

### 2000 — Communities of practice reach the mainstream

Wenger and colleagues promote communities of practice as an organizational form complementing hierarchies and teams. Published in *Harvard Business Review*.

[Source: [Communities of Practice: The Organizational Frontier - HBR, 2000](https://hbr.org/2000/01/communities-of-practice-the-organizational-frontier)]

### 2001 — Wikipedia launches

Wikipedia becomes arguably the most successful knowledge management system ever built — not through formal KM methodology but through open collaboration, coordination mechanisms, and community governance. Its success defies many KM assumptions about needing centralized control and expert authority.

**Historical parallel to AI KM:** Wikipedia proved that distributed, self-organizing knowledge creation could outperform top-down systems — but also showed that success requires sophisticated social infrastructure, not just technology.

[Source: [Harnessing the Wisdom of Crowds in Wikipedia - ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1460563.1460572); [The Crowdsourcing Fallacy - Wikimedia](https://diff.wikimedia.org/2017/10/16/crowdsourcing-fallacy)]

### Early 2000s — KM disillusionment sets in

High-profile failures (PharmaCorp, Engico's Best Practice Tool) demonstrate that technology-first approaches systematically fail. The field enters a "trough of disillusionment."

Root causes identified:
- Poor project implementation
- Misalignment with organizational strategy
- Content deficiencies — problems with creation, capture, and access
- Ignoring the social nature of knowledge

[Source: [PharmaCorp KM Failure - Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/kpm.130); [Best Practice Tool Failure - AIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2006/134/); [KM Project Abandonment Root Causes - AIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol16/iss1/35/)]

### 2001–2005 — Semantic Web ontology era

Ontologies show promise in medical and life sciences (SNOMED, Gene Ontology) but prove costly to develop, hard to get right, and difficult to reuse across domains.

### 2004–2008 — Social software and Web 2.0

Wikis, blogs, social bookmarking, and enterprise social networks emerge as KM tools. The focus shifts from formal systems to informal knowledge sharing.

### 2006–2010 — Linked Data movement

Tim Berners-Lee promotes "Linked Data" principles as a more pragmatic approach to the Semantic Web. Some adoption occurs (DBpedia, government open data) but mainstream uptake remains limited.

## Third Wave: Content and Findability (2010s)

### 2010s — Enterprise search and taxonomy

The focus shifts to making existing knowledge findable through better search, taxonomy construction, and content management. SharePoint, Confluence, and similar platforms dominate.

### 2012 — Google launches the Knowledge Graph

Google's Knowledge Graph represents the most visible success of semantic technologies, powering search result panels for entities. The approach is more practical and less idealistic than the original Semantic Web vision.

### 2010–2014 — JSON-LD and Schema.org

The Semantic Web's most tangible mainstream legacy: Schema.org markup for SEO and JSON-LD as a lightweight linked data format. These succeed by solving narrow, practical problems rather than pursuing grand unified models.

### 2018 — ISO 30401 Knowledge Management Standard

The first international standard for KM systems is published, attempting to formalize KM practice after decades of inconsistent definitions and approaches.

[Source: [Key Milestones in KM Evolution - Springer](https://ideas.repec.org/h/spr/kmochp/978-3-031-38696-1_3.html)]

## The AI KM Era (2020s)

### 2020–2021 — Pandemic accelerates remote KM needs

COVID-19 creates urgent demand for digital knowledge sharing as distributed teams can no longer rely on informal, in-person knowledge transfer. Cloud-based KM solutions see rapid adoption.

### 2022 — ChatGPT launches; LLMs enter mainstream

Large language models demonstrate unprecedented natural language understanding, promising to transform knowledge retrieval from keyword search to conversational interaction.

### 2023 — RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) becomes dominant pattern

RAG emerges as the primary architecture for enterprise AI KM: LLMs augmented with organizational knowledge bases to provide grounded, source-attributed answers. 60% of enterprise LLM systems leverage RAG.

[Source: [RAGOps: Operating and Managing RAG Pipelines - arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03401)]

### 2023–2024 — Enterprise knowledge graphs meet LLMs

Google, Microsoft, and others integrate knowledge graphs with LLM capabilities, creating hybrid systems that combine structured and unstructured knowledge representation.

[Source: [Enterprise Knowledge Graph - Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/enterprise-knowledge-graph/docs); [Industry-Scale Knowledge Graphs - CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/practice/industry-scale-knowledge-graphs/)]

### 2024 — "Knowledge collapse" concern emerges

Researchers warn that recursive reliance on AI-generated knowledge may narrow the distribution of available knowledge, paradoxically making public understanding less accurate. A 20% discount on AI-generated content can generate beliefs 2.3x further from truth.

[Source: [AI and the Problem of Knowledge Collapse - 2024](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240403502P/abstract)]

### 2024–2025 — GRAI framework proposed

Researchers propose the GRAI (Generative AI) framework as a successor to SECI, arguing that generative AI enables machines to represent and process implicit or tacit knowledge through pattern recognition — though critics note this conflates statistical pattern matching with genuine understanding.

[Source: [From SECI to GRAI - ScienceDirect, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2059589125000463)]

### 2025 — KM market reaches estimated $17.3B (software) / $780B+ (overall)

The knowledge management market shows strong growth with 13-18% CAGR, driven by AI integration, remote work, and digital transformation. 58% of vendors have introduced AI-based enhancements.

[Source: [KM Software Market - Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/knowledge-management-software-market); [KM Market Size - Global Growth Insights](https://www.globalgrowthinsights.com/market-reports/knowledge-management-market-116227)]

### 2025–2026 — Enterprise RAG remains largely experimental

Despite enthusiasm, most enterprise RAG deployments remain prototypes. Fewer than 15% of studies address real-time production integration. A significant "lab-to-market" gap persists.

[Source: [RAG and LLMs for Enterprise KM - MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/1/368); [RAG in Industry Interview Study - arXiv, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14066)]

## Recurring Patterns Across Eras

| Pattern | Expert Systems (1980s) | First Wave KM (1990s) | Semantic Web (2000s) | AI KM (2020s) |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|---------------------|---------------|
| Core promise | Capture expert knowledge in rules | Get right info to right people | Machine-readable knowledge web | Intelligent knowledge agents |
| Technology faith | High | High | Very high | Very high |
| Cultural attention | Low | Low initially | Minimal | Growing but still secondary |
| Bottleneck discovered | Knowledge engineering | Knowledge contribution incentives | Ontology creation & maintenance | Data quality, trust, organizational adoption |
| Hype duration | ~6 years | ~8 years | ~10 years | ~3 years so far |
| Practical legacy | Separation of knowledge from inference | Document management systems | Knowledge graphs, Schema.org | TBD |
